Read this before trusting a projection

The model beats both baselines at every position — but by 0.7% overall, and only 2.6% at QB. That is a small edge, and it is the honest number.

Version 1 was 4.4% worse than simply averaging a player's season. It leaned on a four-game recency weighting, and weekly fantasy scoring turns out to be mostly noise. The fix came from sweeping the blend weight against real data instead of tuning by feel: make the season average the anchor, then add a usage term at whatever weight the backtest picks.

So the projection is a commodity. That's precisely why the roadmap spends its real effort on decision leverage — ranking choices by how much they move your odds of winning rather than by projected points. A +2.0 swap in a game you win 88% of the time is worth nothing.

How a projection is built

  1. Every past game is scored with your league's own settings, so format never matters.
  2. The season average anchors the estimate — the hard baseline becomes the base.
  3. An opportunity term (recency-weighted usage × season-long efficiency) blends in at weights the backtest chose: QB 80%, RB 40%, TE 30%, WR 20%.
  4. Injury designations gate it — Out and IR project zero, Questionable takes 15% off.
  5. Floor and ceiling come from the player's own 20th/80th percentile spread.
  6. Confidence combines sample size and role stability: a steady 10-touch back outranks one alternating 2 and 18.
